# 1. What Is Consciousness?

In simple terms, consciousness is each of us as an individual.

In science, however, consciousness has different interpretations across fields and levels of analysis.

Here, our research focuses primarily on **phenomenal consciousness** and **self-awareness**—*whether a system can recognize itself as an object and have subjective experience and agency*.

When does a child begin to develop a sense of “I”?

# 2. Scientific Constraints

All hypotheses here are open to refutation by evidence. Consciousness is treated only as an abstraction of the individual and an object of scientific study; no additional meaning is attributed to it.

# 3. Fundamental Hypothesis

Consciousness is a product of the continuous operation of a particular kind of system and is lost when that system breaks down completely.

# 4. Phenomena Related to Consciousness: Examples

## 1. When You Wake Up, Where Have You Been?

When you wake up, where have you been?

Imagine an ordinary night.

You lie in bed and close your eyes. Before falling asleep, you are still thinking about tomorrow. You can feel the weight of the blanket and the rhythm of your breathing.

Then you lose your continuous sense of time.

When you open your eyes again, it is already eight in the morning.

It feels as though only an instant has passed between closing your eyes last night and opening them today.

Yet eight hours have actually passed.

Throughout those eight hours, your heart kept beating, your body kept regulating its temperature, and your brain continued its complex neural activity.

The physical system never stopped, yet the subjective experience you can remember seems to contain a gap.

But this raises a profound question:

Were you unconscious during some of those eight hours, or did you have experiences that never became memories you could later recall?

If a moment of consciousness really occurred but was then completely forgotten, how could we establish that it ever existed?

In dreams, in particular, you can have another identity, inhabit a space that does not exist, and experience fear and joy. After waking, you know that none of it actually happened, yet the feelings within the dream may have seemed very real at the time.

This forces us to distinguish between the existence of consciousness, its continuity, and its memory of the past.

These may be three different things.

## 2. You Do Not Know Why You Feel What You Feel

Suppose you are studying quietly.

Suddenly, you feel your heart beating faster, your chest tightens, and you feel vaguely uneasy.

You begin to wonder:

“Have I forgotten something important?”

“Is something dangerous happening?”

Yet nothing obvious has changed in your surroundings.

Perhaps you simply drank a lot of coffee, or you are tired, short on sleep, or experiencing hormonal changes.

Clearly, some signal has triggered physiological changes inside your body, and these changes have already begun to affect your judgment of the world.

More intriguingly, even when you know that you are safe, you may not be able to dispel the unease immediately.

Conversely, when you deliberately adjust your breathing, shift your attention, or go for a walk, your bodily state may change again.

A loop emerges:

Bodily changes → Subjective feelings → Cognitive interpretation → Behavior → Further bodily changes

This raises a question that deserves careful thought:

Are emotions your experience of changes in your body, or is your body helping construct the entire world as you experience it?

When you are afraid, the outside world seems dangerous too. When you are happy, the same scene may seem especially beautiful.

The objective environment has not changed to a corresponding degree, but your overall experience of the world has.

## 3. A Smell Takes You Back Ten Years

You walk into an unfamiliar room.

Suddenly, you notice a familiar smell.

It might be the damp smell of a school corridor from your childhood, or the aroma of food in your family's kitchen.

You have not even tried to remember anything.

Yet in an instant, an afternoon you had long forgotten comes back to you.

The sunlight, the sounds, someone's expression, and even feelings that were difficult to describe at the time all seem to return.

Strangely, before that smell appeared, you were not thinking about this experience at all.

It was yours, yet it seemed to lie outside the part of consciousness you could directly access.

Where, then, was this memory before?

And further:

Why can a simple sensory signal suddenly transform your entire subjective world?

One moment, you are dealing with everyday matters in the present; the next, it is as though part of you has returned to childhood.

Perhaps subjective consciousness does not manage memories one by one. Instead, certain neural activities are reactivated by particular cues and reenter ongoing information processing. A signal can trigger a memory, while from the perspective of subjective consciousness, that memory surfaces unbidden. Perhaps this is itself part of how consciousness takes shape.

But there is another question.

The childhood scene you recall may not be entirely accurate. Your brain may fill in details, change the sequence of events, or even incorporate information acquired later into the original memory.

We are therefore using a memory system that is not entirely reliable to construct our understanding of our own past.

This may be related to our sense of continuity in self-awareness.

We feel that we are the same person we were yesterday or ten years ago. But how much does that feeling depend on reconstructing memories?

## 4. Your Body Does Something, but You Did Not Decide Every Step

Suppose you have learned to ride a bicycle.

As you ride, you think about a mathematical problem.

You are not consciously calculating:

How much should the muscles on the left contract now?

At what angle should your body lean?

When should you adjust the handlebars?

Yet your body keeps performing these actions.

Suddenly, an obstacle appears ahead.

You quickly brake and change direction. Only afterward do you clearly realize what just happened.

This is puzzling.

Your body carried out a complex control process, but you did not consciously manage all its details.

At other times, however, you can deliberately decide:

“I want to learn to ride a bicycle.”

Through repeated practice, actions that initially required conscious involvement gradually become automatic.

In other words:

Conscious learning can change neural processes that will later run automatically, while those automatic processes continually support your conscious actions.

There is an analogy here with the layers of a computer operating system. Subconscious processes and muscle memory clearly operate at a different level from subjective consciousness. You cannot directly issue commands to your cells: your subjective consciousness has no access to that level of control. In the analogy, you lack the necessary permissions; those underlying processes are inaccessible to you. Yet you can alter them indirectly through physical intervention—for example, by performing surgery on yourself. (It is as though some powerful designer had set your access permissions in advance to keep you safe.)

Now take this further:

You have learned to ride a bicycle, but later lose certain motor abilities because of a brain disorder.

You still consciously want to ride and still know how, but parts of your nervous system can no longer execute the necessary movements.

This shows that:

Wanting to perform an action;

Knowing how to perform it;

Actually performing it;

can come apart.

We therefore need to ask:

Is consciousness the part of the nervous system that issues commands, or is it an overall state that arises when different systems work together?

There is currently no simple answer.

## 5. You Are Studying Your Own Brain, but Cannot Directly See How Your Thoughts Form

Right now, you are reading this text.

Try something:

Think of any number.

Suppose you think of 37.

Then ask yourself:

“Why 37, of all numbers?”

You might offer an explanation: it seems fairly random, or you have encountered it before.

But did you actually observe all the neural processes that produced that number?

No.

What you observed was a result that had already entered consciousness, along with your own explanation of it.

You do not know exactly which neurons were involved, which memories were activated, or which chemical changes influenced your choice.

More subtly, you can keep asking:

“Why did I just come up with that explanation?”

You then begin thinking about your own thinking.

This creates a very unusual relationship:

The system produces cognition → Cognition attempts to explain the system

But this does not establish that consciousness can never understand its own physical basis.

Humans have developed neuroimaging techniques and studied synapses, neurotransmitters, electrical activity, and brain structure.

We can even use brain–computer interfaces, neuromodulation, and drugs to alter our own neural activity.

In other words, cognition that arises within a system can, through scientific tools and interaction with the external world, act back on the physical substrate that produces it.

Consciousness and its substrate are therefore related. Consciousness does not inherently have complete information about its own substrate, but it can gradually investigate it.

Consider a deeper question:

Suppose you think about the same problem on two consecutive days.

On the first day, you are well rested. Your mind feels clear, and you can quickly connect several concepts.

On the second day, you are severely fatigued. You can understand each concept individually, but struggle to bring them together. You may even feel unable to keep thinking.

Your knowledge has not necessarily disappeared, and your goal has not changed.

Yet the physical system you depend on is in a different state, changing your entire cognitive process.

For humans, then, might consciousness be a changing process that continually depends on the physical activity of the nervous system, rather than a fixed “thing”?

We do not know.

## 6. When You Feel Pain, Why Does the Nervous System Not Only Detect Injury and Organize Avoidance, but Also Produce a Subjective Feeling of “Hurting”?

Suppose a system can detect injury, accurately assess danger, form memories, take action, and even avoid the same stimulus in the future.

Beyond performing all these functions, why is that irreducible subjective experience needed—or why does it arise at all?
